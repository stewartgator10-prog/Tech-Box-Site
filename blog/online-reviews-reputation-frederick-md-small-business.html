Reputation

Online Reviews Are Quietly Deciding Who Frederick, MD Customers Call First

By Tech Box Solutions · October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Before a customer ever reads your website, calls your shop, or walks through your door, there's a good chance they've already read what other people had to say about you. A quick search, a glance at the stars, a skim of the last few comments — and in seconds, they've half-made up their mind.

That's the part a lot of small business owners miss. You can have a great website and a sharp Google Business Profile, and still lose the job because your reviews are thin, old, or never answered. Reputation isn't a side project anymore — it's doing real work in every buying decision, especially for local, in-person services. Here's what the research says, and how a business in Frederick County can actually get more good reviews without feeling salesy about it.

1. Reviews come before almost everything else

By the time most people reach out, they've already done their homework. Survey research on consumer review habits consistently finds that the large majority of shoppers read online reviews before they visit or buy from a local business, and that Google is where most of that reading happens.[1]

That means your reviews are often the very first impression of your business — before your homepage, before your "About Us" page, before anyone sees your work in person. If that first impression is a handful of old, mediocre reviews, you're starting every potential job at a disadvantage you don't even know about.

2. Most people will leave a review — if you ask

Here's the good news: customers aren't reluctant to leave reviews. They're just rarely asked at the right moment. Industry research has found that around 70% of consumers will leave a review for a business when asked to.[2] The gap isn't willingness — it's that most small businesses never make the ask part of how they do business.

And people don't stop at one site. A large share of shoppers check at least two different review sources — Google, Facebook, Yelp, or an industry-specific site — before deciding who to call.[1] If your reviews are strong on Google but nonexistent everywhere else, that's a gap worth closing.

3. How you respond matters as much as what people say

A negative review isn't the end of the world — ignoring one is closer to it. Research on review behavior shows that roughly 53% of consumers expect a business to respond to a negative review within a week.[3] A calm, professional reply often does more to build trust with future customers than the original complaint does to hurt you. Silence, on the other hand, reads as confirmation.

of consumers expect a business to respond to a negative review within a week.[3]

The same goes for the good reviews. A quick "thank you" reply costs nothing and shows every future reader that a real person is paying attention on the other end.

4. A simple system beats hoping it happens naturally

Most owners know reviews matter. Very few have an actual system for getting them. A few things that consistently work for small, local businesses:

  • Ask at the best moment — right after the job is done, the invoice is paid, or the customer says thanks, not weeks later in a generic email blast.
  • Make it a one-tap link — a direct link to your Google review form by text message converts far better than asking someone to "look us up and leave a review."
  • Respond to every review — good and bad, briefly and professionally. It takes minutes and it's visible to every future customer who reads it.
  • Never fake it — purchased or incentivized reviews violate Google's policies and can get your whole profile penalized. Real reviews, asked for honestly, are the only ones worth having.

This is exactly the kind of small, repeatable process that's easy to build once and then forget about — a text goes out automatically after a job closes, a dashboard shows you what came in, and you spend your time doing the work instead of chasing feedback.

The bottom line

In a market like Frederick, where plenty of good businesses are competing for the same searches, your reviews are often the tiebreaker. Two companies can offer the same service at the same price — the one with 40 recent, five-star reviews and thoughtful replies wins the call almost every time. Treating your reputation as something you actively manage, rather than something that just happens to you, is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost moves a small business can make.
