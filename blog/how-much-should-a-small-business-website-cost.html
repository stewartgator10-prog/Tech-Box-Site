How Much Should a Small Business Website Really Cost?

By Tech Box Solutions · September 14, 2026 · 6 min read

Ask five people what a website should cost and you'll get five wildly different answers. A cousin built one on a $15-a-month builder over a weekend. A neighbor paid a freelancer $3,000. A friend in another town got quoted $30,000 by an agency. None of them are lying — they're just describing different products wearing the same name.

If you're trying to budget for a website and the range of answers feels useless, it's not you. The market really is that scattered. Here's what actually drives the price, where the hidden costs hide, and how to tell if a quote is fair.

1. There are really three different products, not one

"Website" covers everything from a template you drag text into, to a fully custom site built around how your business actually works. Roughly speaking, small businesses end up in one of three lanes:

DIY builders (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy) typically run $15 to $50 a month for a standard site, up to $30 to $300 a month if you need online sales. A basic WordPress DIY setup can start even cheaper — $100 to $300 up front plus $10 to $50 a month for hosting.[1]

Freelancers and small studios building a custom site generally charge $2,000 to $8,000 up front, depending on how many pages and features you need.[1]

Agencies land at $10,000 to $35,000, with complex e-commerce builds starting around $20,000 and climbing from there.[1][2]

the typical range for a professionally built, custom small-business website.[1]

None of these is automatically "right." A one-page site for a new sole proprietor doesn't need agency money behind it. But a business that lives and dies on its website — booking jobs, taking leads, closing sales — usually outgrows a $20-a-month template fast.

2. The sticker price is never the whole bill

The number you're quoted up front is rarely the number you'll actually spend. Every website carries ongoing costs that don't show up until the invoices start arriving: domain renewal ($10 to $20 a year), hosting ($5 to $15 a month for shared hosting, more for higher-traffic sites), and SSL certificates, which are cheap but disable your whole site if you let them lapse.[3]

Then there's maintenance — the security patches, software updates, backups, and small content changes that keep a site running and safe. For a small business, that typically lands somewhere between $50 and $500 a month depending on how complex the site is and who's doing the work.[3]

$1,100–$5,000
a year in hosting, security, backups, and maintenance most owners don't budget for up front.[1]

None of this is a reason to avoid investing in a website — it's a reason to ask about it before you sign anything. A cheap build with an expensive, unclear maintenance plan behind it can end up costing more over five years than a well-built site with honest pricing from day one.[4]

3. What actually moves the price

Once you're past the basic template tier, cost comes down to a short list of things: how many pages and how much custom design work, whether you need booking or scheduling built in, whether the site needs to talk to other software you use (like a CRM or payment processor), and how much of the writing and photography you're providing versus asking someone to create. A five-page site with your own photos costs a lot less than a fifteen-page site with custom booking, before you've paid for a single extra feature.

This is also where a lot of small businesses get burned by low quotes. A rock-bottom price usually means a template with your logo dropped in, no real strategy behind the page layout, and little to no support once it's live. It looks like savings on the invoice and shows up later as a site that doesn't rank on Google, doesn't work well on a phone, or breaks the first time you need to change something.

What this looks like for a Frederick, MD business

In a market like Frederick County, most of your competitors fall into that DIY-or-nothing category — plenty of local businesses are still running on an outdated template or no site at all. That's an opening, not a warning. A clean, well-built site in the $2,000–$8,000 range is realistic for most local service and retail businesses here, and it's usually enough to stand out from the competitors who haven't invested in theirs at all.

The goal isn't to spend the most. It's to spend appropriately for what your business actually needs, with a builder who's upfront about what happens after launch — not just what it costs to go live.

The bottom line

A fair website price depends entirely on what you're buying and what comes after. Get specific: how many pages, what features, who hosts it, what happens when something breaks, and what the honest year-two cost looks like — not just the number on the proposal. A builder who won't answer those questions plainly is telling you something.
